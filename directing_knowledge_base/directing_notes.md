# PART 0 — HOW TO USE THIS DOCUMENT

There are only **two levels of decision**:

1. **Once per film** — eight house rules about the whole film (Part 2). Every scene inherits them.
2. **Once per scene** — run the scene down the 18-step Staircase (Part 3). The camera comes *last*, on purpose.

The golden rule: **start at the desk with meaning, reach the camera only at the end.** Most beginners think about shots first. Steps 1–9 decide what the scene *means* before any shot is chosen, so that by the time I reach the camera I'm making precise technical choices in service of decisions already made — not choosing from infinite options under pressure on set.

### Handing a scene to my AI partner

When I upload a scene (script or visual screenplay), the assistant runs it down the Staircase and returns a filled-in **Scene Directing Card** (the blank form lives in `scene_directing_card.md`). The card:

- forces every visual choice back to the dramatic spine,
- names which tools it's using by number (e.g. "applying Tool 6 + Tool 12"),
- ends with **questions for me**, not commands — I keep authorship.

### A note to myself

Everything here should be readable by me **on set at 6am**. If any part ever reads like a technical manual instead of a director's notebook, that's a bug — fix it.

# PART 2 — THE MACRO PASS (decide once per film)

Before any single scene, answer these eight questions about the film as a whole. They become the house rules every scene inherits, so I'm not re-inventing the look each day.

1. **What is this film about, in one sentence?** (the controlling idea under everything)
2. **Whose story is it?** Whose eyes are we behind — and does that ever shift? *(Tool 03)*
3. **What's the emotional temperature map?** Hot / cold / quiet, scene by scene, across the whole thing. *(Tool 27)*
4. **Where are the detonations?** Name the 1–2 (short) / 3–5 (feature) suspense peaks everything builds toward *(Tool 01)*, and the 2–3 (short) / 8–12 (feature) counterpoint punctuation moments *(Tool 06)*.
5. **What's the colour and light language?** Warm / cool / shadow. *(Tool 09)*
6. **What's the lens language?** Wide and uneasy, or long and watchful? *(Tool 13)*
7. **What shape is the frame?** Aspect ratio as a deliberate emotional choice.
8. **What's the sound philosophy?** Lots of score, or trust silence? Diegetic bias? *(Tools 08, 34)*

Write these down once per project. Every scene below just *refers* to them.

# PART 3 — THE STAIRCASE (the per-scene workflow)

Run every scene top to bottom. Each step narrows the next. Steps 1–9 are at the desk (reading). Steps 10–18 move toward and onto the set, then into the edit.

### — Desk: what the scene means —

**Step 1 — What does each character WANT here?**
The dramatic spine. My writer's home turf. Everything visual hangs off this. *(Tool 30)*

**Step 2 — What's STOPPING them?**
The obstacle. No obstacle, no scene.

**Step 3 — What CHANGES by the end?**
If nothing changes, the scene is probably dead time.

**Step 4 — What JOB does the scene do for the film?**
Establishment / Loading / Suspense / Release. *(S1-L2, Tool 01)*

**Step 5 — What TYPE of scene is it?**
Dialogue with action / Dialogue only / Action only. This tells me which tools even apply. *(S1-L1)*

**Step 6 — What are the TWO JOBS it must do at once?**
No dead scenes. If it's only doing one thing, find the second. *(Tool 21)*

**Step 7 — Whose HEAD are we inside?**
Name the one consciousness the camera belongs to. If unclear, the audience has no one to hold. *(Tool 03)*

**Step 8 — What does the scene WANT TO BE, in one sentence?**
Its visual DNA. The bridge from script to image. E.g. *"A warm room that slowly feels like a trap."* *(Tool 28)*

**Step 9 — Where do we ENTER and LEAVE?**
Latest possible moment in; earliest possible moment out. Let imagination fill both ends. *(Tool 26)*

### — Desk: how to shoot it —

**Step 10 — What's the BORING version we must avoid?**
Name the obvious, default-coverage way to shoot this. Then beat it (unless default genuinely serves the scene). This is where style is built.

**Step 11 — What's each actor's PLAYABLE ACTION?**
Give a verb, not a feeling: *to hide, to test, to seduce, to punish, to escape.* Keeps performance active. *(Tool 29)*

**Step 12 — What's the ONE main tool making the meaning?**
Pick a single engine: Show don't tell / Counterpoint / Deep space / Kuleshov / Sound against image / Colour. *(Tools 05, 06, 08, 09, 10, 15)*

**Step 13 — What's the single most important BEAT?**
The one moment that matters above all. Everything before it stays wide; here is where I cut in close. *(Tool 23)*

**Step 14 — How are the BODIES staged?**
Who moves, who's still (movement = power). Singles vs two-shots. Camera height per person. Doorways / windows / mirrors. Plus the basics: thirds, headroom, lead room. *(Tools 22, 12, 11, 31)*

**Step 15 — What's the LOOK?** *(pulled from the Macro Pass)*
Lens for this scene. Does the camera move (locked / slow push / handheld / oner) and *why*? Colour and light note for this location. *(Tools 13, 10, 32, 09)*

### — Shot list & edit —

**Step 16 — The SHOT LIST (last desk step).**
Wide to establish the map, break to close at the important beat. Tag each shot **information** or **emotion**. Justify every close-up. Keep the camera on one side of the line; watch eyelines; plan cut-on-action. *(Tools 14, 23, 24, 25, 33)*

**Step 17 — How should it CUT?** *(for the edit)*
Reaction shots — whose, when, how long. Hold or cut (hold past comfort). Rhythm. Transition in/out. Sound pass — dialogue clear? Is silence already doing the work before any music? J-cuts and L-cuts. *(Tools 18, 19, 16, 17, 20, 08, 34)*

**Step 18 — What's the DIRECTOR'S BET?**
One sentence: *"The scene works if the audience feels ___ before they understand ___."* This records what I was trying to make the audience experience — and over time it teaches me my own style.

### Worked example (running a real scene down the Staircase)

Script: *"Meera comes home. Her husband Ravi is sitting in the dark. They talk about her day. She lies."*

1. **Want:** Meera wants to hide where she's been; Ravi wants to see if she'll lie.
2. **Obstacle:** Neither will say it out loud.
3. **Change:** She realises he already knows.
4. **Job:** Loading — planting the rupture that detonates later.
5. **Type:** Dialogue only (hardest — burden on staging and faces).
6. **Two jobs:** Deliver the small talk *and* quietly shift power to Ravi.
7. **Whose head:** Meera's — we feel her exposure.
8. **Wants to be:** *"A familiar living room that closes like a fist."*
9. **Enter/leave:** Enter mid-sentence; cut away the instant she realises he knows — before either reacts.
10. **Boring version:** Flat shot/reverse-shot at eye level. Avoid.
11. **Playable actions:** Meera — *to conceal*. Ravi — *to test*.
12. **Main tool:** Counterpoint — warm chatter on top, dread underneath.
13. **Key beat:** the half-second her smile falls.
14. **Bodies:** Ravi still and seated (control); Meera moving, then frozen. Singles. Ravi slightly low (power), Meera slightly high (exposed).
15. **Look:** longer lens (surveillance); locked-off camera (no rescue); cool light creeping into a warm room.
16. **Shots:** wide of the room first (the map) → stay wide through small talk → close-up of Meera exactly at the smile-drop.
17. **Cut:** hold that close-up two beats too long; no music — let ticking-clock silence carry it; cut before resolution.
18. **Director's bet:** *The scene works if the audience feels the trap close before they understand that Ravi already knows.*

Notice the camera choices (15–16) were almost automatic by the time I reached them — because steps 1–14 already decided what the scene meant.

## THE ORIGINAL FRAMEWORK (S1)

> Preserved from the original document. This is the seed of the Staircase in Part 3.

**Four questions — answered in sequence before calling any shot.**

Before calling any shot, a director answers four questions in order. Each layer narrows the decisions so that by the time you reach the camera you are making precise technical choices in service of decisions already made — not choosing from infinite options under pressure on set.

- **Layer 1 — Scene type.** Dialogue with action — characters speaking while something physical is also happening. Dialogue without action — pure conversation. Entire burden on staging, blocking, shot choice and rhythm. Hardest to direct. Action without dialogue — pure cinema. Character and emotion carried entirely by image, movement and sound.
- **Layer 2 — Emotional architecture.** Establishment — building who the character is and why we care. The longer you do this, the harder suspense sequences hit. Loading — appearing to be about one thing while secretly placing the conditions for the detonation. Should be invisible as loading. Suspense — the detonation. Rare and spaced. 3–5 per feature, 1–2 per short.
- **Layer 3 — Primary tool.** Show don't tell / Counterpoint / Deep space / Kuleshov cut — which tool is making meaning in this scene?
- **Layer 4 — Shot grammar.** Information shot or emotion shot. The close-up — justify it emotionally. The cut — know what the collision creates. Hold the shot past comfort.

**Rule ▸** Decide layers 1, 2 and 3 in prep. Arrive on set only making layer 4 decisions.

## CATEGORY A — AUDIENCE PSYCHOLOGY

### 01 · Suspense vs. surprise
*Category: Audience Psychology · Use when: you want sustained tension instead of a momentary shock.*

Surprise is a bomb that explodes without warning — one moment of shock, then it is over. Suspense is showing the audience the bomb before the scene begins and playing an ordinary scene over it. Same material, completely different experience. The difference is information management. Suspense requires the audience to know more than the characters. That gap creates tension.

**Rule ▸** Never withhold information to create tension. Give it to the audience early and let the gap do the work.

**The three levers — stakes comes first**
- **Stakes** — The foundation. Establish what the character stands to lose before any tension arrives. Without this, nothing else functions.
- **Time** — The pressure valve. A visible deadline makes every delay and obstacle felt physically.
- **Uncertainty** — Pure uncertainty with no stakes is mystery. Certainty about outcome with uncertain direction becomes dread — often more powerful than suspense.

**Scene architecture — establishment, loading, suspense**
- **Establishment** — Who are these people, what do they want, why do we care. No suspense. The more carefully you do this, the harder your suspense sequences hit.
- **Loading** — Appears ordinary while secretly placing the conditions for detonation. Can hide inside comedy, romance or exposition. Should be invisible as loading.
- **Suspense** — The detonation. Only works because everything before it has been doing its job. 3–5 per feature, 1–2 per short film.

### 02 · The three-way game
*Category: Audience Psychology · Use when: checking any decision — what will the audience actually feel here?*

Filmmaking is not a conversation between director and picture. It is a three-way game in which the audience is also required to play. The film is not finished when you cut it — it is finished when an audience watches it and assembles meaning from it. Every decision — what to show, what to withhold, when to cut, how long to hold — is made in relationship to that absent third party. Show don't tell only works because an audience completes the image. Counterpoint only works because an audience feels the gap. The cut only works because an audience assembles meaning between shots. The audience is the instrument you are playing. Directors who forget this make self-expression. Directors who remember it make cinema.

**Rule ▸** After designing any scene ask: what will an audience member feel at this exact moment — not understand, feel?

### 03 · Controlling sympathy — whose story is this?
*Category: Audience Psychology · Use when: deciding whose consciousness the camera lives inside.*

The audience will inhabit whatever consciousness the director places them inside through camera position, information management and editing. This can be established, maintained and transferred deliberately across the film. Psycho is the most extreme example — 45 minutes in Marion's story, then her murder, then a complete transfer of sympathy to Norman Bates. The audience finds themselves inside the perspective of a killer and cannot get out. That transfer is engineered through specific directing decisions: whose point of view the camera adopts, what information the audience has that characters don't, whose face we are in close-up when decisions are made.

**Rule ▸** Before shooting any scene, name whose consciousness the camera is inside. If the answer is unclear, the audience has no one to inhabit.

### 04 · The false plateau — engineering false release
*Category: Audience Psychology · Use when: a suspense sequence runs longer than two minutes.*

Audiences need to believe a tension has resolved before the next detonation can hit at full force. A suspense sequence that escalates continuously without apparent release produces numbness. The false plateau gives the audience a moment of apparent safety — the threat seems to have passed, the scene appears to be winding down — and then detonates again from a new direction. The relief makes the audience vulnerable. That vulnerability is what the next peak exploits. Hitchcock uses this in almost every major suspense sequence — the birds appear to have gone, Melanie relaxes, then the chimney erupts.

**Rule ▸** In any suspense sequence longer than two minutes, engineer at least one false plateau. Let the audience breathe — then take the air away again.

### 05 · Show don't tell
*Category: Audience Psychology / baseline · Use when: always. This is the baseline for every moment.*

Show don't tell is not a technique deployed in certain scenes — it is the baseline standard for every moment without exception. The camera can film thought, jealousy, desire, suspicion and fear directly. Every time a character announces what they feel through dialogue, the director has failed to find the visual equivalent. The discipline is to strip every scene back to what can be communicated purely through image, movement and sound — and then ensure any remaining dialogue is doing counterpoint work rather than explanation.

**Rule ▸** After writing a scene, go line by line. Every piece of dialogue that names or explains an emotion — cut it and find the image instead.

**Adapting for Tamil audiences**
Tamil cinema has a tradition of dialogue as craft — audiences come to hear things said beautifully as much as to see things shown. The goal is not to make a silent European art film. The goal is to ensure that when characters speak, dialogue is doing counterpoint work — saying one thing while the image carries another. Let the dialogue be beautiful. Make the image work simultaneously. Tamil audiences read faces, bodies and space with enormous sophistication — they simply have not always been asked to. The assumption that Indian audiences need everything explained is a lazy production habit, not a cultural truth.

**Rule ▸** Never eliminate dialogue — elevate it. When characters speak, the image must carry a simultaneous different meaning.

## CATEGORY B — MEANING-MAKING

### 06 · Counterpoint
*Category: Meaning-making · Use when: a character says one thing but feels another.*

Counterpoint is distinct from show don't tell. Show don't tell subtracts — remove the explanation, trust the image. Counterpoint adds — keep the dialogue but run the image simultaneously as a separate contradicting stream. Two things happening at once create a third meaning in the gap. In real life people almost never say what they feel. Dialogue is social performance. The truth lives in the eyes, the body, the involuntary movements.

- **Maximum contrast** — Surface is mundane and ordinary. Subtext is extreme — betrayal, desire, a decision to harm. The gap between them creates unbearable tension.
- **Rhyming subtext** — Surface subject secretly mirrors the real subject. When surface and subtext rhyme, the scene works below the audience's conscious awareness.

**Grammar vs punctuation — how many scenes**
As grammar — present in most dialogue scenes as baseline human reality. Characters always manage their self-presentation. As punctuation — fully designed moments where the gap is so wide the audience consciously feels the tension between the two layers. A feature holds 8–12 punctuation moments. A short film holds 2–3.

Calibration for 2026 — show the gaze as long as needed, remove every editorial comment around it. No music swell. No confirming reaction shot. Trust that they caught it.

**Rule ▸** Write dialogue about one thing. Let the visuals carry something else entirely. The gap between them is the emotion.

### 07 · Film the thought, not the statement
*Category: Meaning-making · Use when: placing the camera in any scene — follow attention, not dialogue.*

Before placing the camera, ask: where is this character's attention actually going right now? Not where the dialogue suggests. Not where a polite observer would look. Where is the mind actually pointed? The camera follows that. Consciousness is almost never paying attention to what it is supposed to be paying attention to. A character talks about one thing while their eyes and attention are entirely elsewhere. The camera that follows the eyes rather than the conversation is filming actual human experience. This is what makes Hitchcock a realist.

**Rule ▸** In every scene, identify where each character's attention is actually directed. Ask whether the camera is following the truth or the performance.

### 08 · Sound as counterpoint
*Category: Meaning-making · Use when: deciding whether (and how) sound should run with or against the image.*

Sound and image are two separate streams that can be made to run together or against each other. When they run together — music tells you how to feel about what you're seeing — the director has done the audience's emotional work for them. This is the most overused crutch in cinema. When sound runs against the image — a violent scene played with gentle ambient sound, a tender scene with harsh background noise — the gap between them creates the same third meaning as visual counterpoint. The schoolhouse scene in The Birds: children singing inside, birds accumulating in silence outside. Two completely opposing emotional registers playing simultaneously. The cheerful singing makes the birds worse.

**Rule ▸** Before adding any music to a scene, ask what the silence is already doing. Music fills the space before the audience gets to feel it themselves.

### 09 · Colour and light as emotional language
*Category: Meaning-making · Use when: setting the look of the film and each location.*

Colour and light are not aesthetic preferences — they are narrative decisions. A director who has a colour language for their film is communicating below the audience's conscious awareness continuously. Warm desaturated tones read as memory or nostalgia. Cool blue reads as institutional power or emotional distance. Deep shadow reads as moral ambiguity. Overexposure reads as memory or trauma. These associations are not arbitrary — they are culturally embedded and physiologically real. Wong Kar-wai, Fincher and PTA all make colour decisions that are inseparable from narrative decisions.

**Rule ▸** Decide the colour and light language of your film before you shoot. It should be as deliberate as the dialogue.

### 10 · Deep space composition + the Spielberg oner
*Category: Meaning-making / Composition · Use when: two things could share a frame instead of being cut between.*

Deep space composition places two simultaneous emotional streams in the same frame at different depths — foreground and background — without cutting. The audience chooses where to look, which creates the feeling of observed reality rather than constructed cinema. The two planes comment on each other without the director explicitly connecting them. For short films with limited locations it is especially valuable — a single well-constructed deep space shot carries the work of several cuts.

The Spielberg oner extends this into movement — a continuous moving master shot where the camera travels through three-dimensional layered space, revealing new information as it goes. Characters talk while the world around them keeps happening. It combines deep space, continuous revelation and counterpoint simultaneously — conversation happening while something else entirely is visible in the surrounding world.

**Rule ▸** Before cutting between two things, ask whether they can share a frame. What does their simultaneous presence create that a cut would not?

## CATEGORY C — COMPOSITION

### 11 · Frame within a frame + reflections
*Category: Composition · Use when: you want to trap, isolate, double, or imply an observer.*

A frame within a frame uses an element inside the image — a doorway, window, arch, gap between bodies — to create a second border around the subject. It creates depth, communicates psychological entrapment or isolation, and implies an observer in the framing position even when no one is physically there. Rear Window is almost entirely shot as frame-within-frame — the window always present, the audience always the observer.

Reflections do something distinct — they create doubling. The character and their reflection are both present, raising the question of which is the performed self and which is the inner self. A mirror can show the audience what a character cannot see behind them — the threat in the reflection they haven't noticed. This compresses the Kuleshov cut into a single image: character and what they should fear visible simultaneously.

Welles' mirror room in Lady from Shanghai is the extreme limit — frame within frame within frame, reflections multiplying infinitely, identity completely dissolved.

**Rule ▸** Doorways, windows and mirrors are never just architecture. Every frame within a frame is a statement about who is contained, observed or doubled.

### 12 · Camera height and power
*Category: Composition · Use when: introducing a character or marking a shift in power.*

Camera height is one of the most immediate and most underused tools a director has. Below eye level — the subject has weight, dominance, gravity. The Godfather shoots Brando almost always slightly below eye level — he feels like a natural force. Above eye level — the subject is diminished, vulnerable, observed, controlled. Eye level — neutral, democratic, journalistic. Most directors default to eye level for everything. Deliberate variation runs a constant quiet argument underneath every scene about who matters, who has power and who is being judged.

**Rule ▸** For every significant character introduction, decide their camera height deliberately. That height should be consistent until the power dynamic shifts — then change it.

### 13 · Lens choice as emotional temperature
*Category: Composition · Use when: setting the feel of a scene before any actor moves.*

Lens choice is a pre-production decision that changes everything about how a scene feels before a single actor moves. A wide lens distorts space, exaggerates distances, creates unease and puts the character in active relationship to their environment — they cannot be separated from where they are. A long lens compresses space, isolates the subject from their background, creates the feeling of surveillance or of intimacy — the subject does not know they are being watched this closely. A wide lens says: this person is defined by their world. A long lens says: this person is being observed.

**Rule ▸** Decide the lens language of each scene before arriving on set. Wide for entrapment in environment. Long for surveillance and isolation.

### 14 · Information shots vs emotion shots
*Category: Composition · Use when: deciding what job each shot is doing.*

Every shot has one primary job — make the audience understand something, or make them feel something. Information shots orient: wide shots establishing geography, mid shots showing spatial relationships, inserts showing relevant objects. Emotion shots feel: the close-up of a face, a hand, an object with emotional weight. Know which job each shot is doing before calling it. Information and emotion have a natural rhythm — orientation, depth, advance, landing. Most coverage-based directors mix these randomly.

No other medium has the cut — two images in direct collision creating a third meaning that exists in neither image alone. No other medium has the close-up in the same way — a human face filling the entire visual field, closer than real life allows except in moments of extreme intimacy or conflict. These two tools belong to cinema alone. Every other element — dialogue, performance, sets, music — other media also have.

**Rule ▸** Every time you use a close-up, justify it emotionally. Every time you cut, know what the collision between these two specific images creates.

### 15 · The Kuleshov cut — look / see / react
*Category: Composition / Cutting · Use when: an action-without-dialogue scene needs to put us inside a head.*

The look-see-react sequence is the most direct route into a character's consciousness that cinema has. Three shots, no dialogue, audience inside someone's head. Shot 1: the character looking. Shot 2: what they see. Shot 3: their reaction. The Kuleshov effect proves that shot 3 — the same neutral face — reads as completely different emotions depending entirely on what you place in shot 2. The face does not change. The meaning does. This is the closest thing cinema has to a superpower.

- **Shot 1 — the look** — Hold long enough to establish directed attention. Short enough to create urgency.
- **Shot 2 — what they see** — The most important shot. Controls the meaning of shot 3 entirely. Exact framing, angle and duration are your argument.
- **Shot 3 — the reaction** — Hold longer than comfortable. The information lands quickly. The emotion builds in the held beat after. This is where Hitchcock is most distinctive.

The mirror shot compresses the entire Kuleshov sequence into a single frame — character and what they see (or what is behind them) visible simultaneously. Spielberg uses this constantly. De Palma built a career on it.

**Rule ▸** In any action-without-dialogue scene, the Kuleshov cut is your primary language. Shot 2 is the most important decision you make.

## CATEGORY D — CUTTING & TIME

### 16 · Rhythmic montage / impact editing
*Category: Cutting & Time · Use when: you want physical sensation, not just meaning.*

Editing rhythm creates physical sensation in the audience — not just meaning. Fast cutting raises heart rate. Rhythmic cutting to sound creates a near-musical physical experience. The Psycho shower scene has 70+ cuts in 45 seconds — but almost none show what you think they show. You never see the knife penetrate skin. The violence is assembled entirely in the audience's head between cuts. The editing implies the act without depicting it — which makes it more violent than anything that could be shown directly. Scorsese in Raging Bull extends this into slow motion and sound design — the ring becomes an almost abstract space, the cuts stop being informational and become physiological.

This is distinct from the Kuleshov cut — that is about meaning assembled between shots. Rhythmic montage is about sensation created by the rhythm and collision of shots. Contemporary examples: Mad Max Fury Road chase sequences, the opening of Dunkirk, the Oldboy corridor fight.

**Rule ▸** Rhythmic montage implies more than it shows. The audience's construction of what happened between cuts is always more powerful than what you could depict directly.

### 17 · Time manipulation — freeze, slow motion, speed
*Category: Cutting & Time · Use when: externalising a character's subjective experience of a moment.*

Cinema can distort clock time to externalise subjective experience. The freeze frame arrests a moment of peak subjective significance — the world literally stops because this moment is so complete. Scorsese uses it when a character arrives somewhere they have always wanted to be, making the audience feel the arrested time of that arrival. Used at a film's end, the freeze refuses the finality of a cut to black — the image keeps existing, suspended (400 Blows, Thelma and Louise).

Slow motion aestheticises its subject — which is morally complex when the subject is violence. Raging Bull uses slow motion to make brutality almost beautiful, then implicates the audience in having watched it carefully. Speed changes create comic distance or dreamlike unreality. All three tools share one principle: they signal that clock time and subjective time have separated.

**Rule ▸** Use time manipulation to externalise a character's subjective experience of a moment — not for stylistic decoration. Ask what the distorted time is telling the audience about how this moment feels from inside.

### 18 · The reaction shot
*Category: Cutting & Time · Use when: a dialogue scene needs its most powerful editorial beat.*

The decision of when to cut to a reaction, whose reaction to cut to, and how long to hold it is one of the most powerful editorial tools in a dialogue scene. Cutting to the wrong person's reaction in a crowd scene completely changes what the scene means. Cutting too early robs the previous shot of its weight. Holding a reaction shot past the point of information delivery — into the territory where the audience is completing the emotion themselves — is where the most powerful moments in cinema live. The reaction shot is the Kuleshov cut's third element isolated as a standalone tool.

**Rule ▸** Before cutting to any reaction shot, ask: whose reaction matters most right now — and what happens if I hold it five seconds longer than feels comfortable?

### 19 · The held shot vs the cut
*Category: Cutting & Time · Use when: deciding whether to relieve tension or refuse relief.*

Cutting relieves tension momentarily — it gives the audience a micro-reset, a new point of orientation. Sometimes the most powerful choice is to refuse that relief. The long take creates real time — the audience cannot be rescued by a cut. They are locked in the scene with the character, experiencing duration as pressure. The decision of when not to cut is as important as the decision of when to cut. Most beginner directors cut too early — out of nervousness, out of habit, out of a feeling that something should be happening. Holding a shot longer than comfortable is one of the most powerful and simplest tools available.

**Rule ▸** Cut later than feels comfortable. The beat after information lands — where nothing new is happening — is where imagination activates and emotion builds.

### 20 · Scene transitions — how films move between scenes
*Category: Cutting & Time · Use when: deciding the relationship between two adjacent scenes.*

The transition between scenes is itself a directing decision carrying distinct emotional meaning.

- **Hard cut** — Immediate, jarring or neutral depending on context. The default. Creates the sharpest collision between two scenes — use when you want the juxtaposition to be felt.
- **Smash cut** — A hard cut from peak intensity — mid-action, mid-sound — to something completely different. Creates shock, disorientation, dark comedy.
- **Match cut** — A cut where a visual element in shot A is echoed in shot B — shape, movement, colour, action. Creates powerful thematic connection across time and space. Kubrick's bone to spaceship in 2001 is the most famous.
- **Jump cut** — A cut within the same scene that removes time — creating a lurch forward. Godard used it as emotional rupture in Breathless. Creates restlessness, anxiety, the feeling of time being eaten.
- **Dissolve** — One image fades into another while overlapping. Communicates time passing, memory, dream states. Softens the transition — the two scenes briefly coexist.
- **Fade to black** — Complete closure — this chapter is over. Used sparingly, a fade to black carries enormous weight. Overused, it becomes punctuation without meaning.

**Rule ▸** Every transition between scenes is a statement about the relationship between those two scenes. Never choose a transition by default.

## CATEGORY E — SCENE & FILM ARCHITECTURE

### 21 · No dead time — every scene must do two things
*Category: Scene & Film Architecture · Use when: planning any scene, especially "connective tissue."*

There is no such thing as a neutral scene. Every moment of connective tissue — every transition, logistical beat, expository exchange — is an opportunity to introduce a disturbing note, deepen a character, shift a power dynamic or plant a visual motif that will activate later. Most directors treat exposition and transition scenes as problems to get through. Hitchcock treats them as free real estate. A scene that is only doing one job is a scene that is not doing its job.

**Rule ▸** Before shooting any scene, name two things it must accomplish simultaneously. If you can only name one, find the second before you shoot it.

### 22 · Blocking as argument — singles vs two-shots
*Category: Scene & Film Architecture · Use when: deciding how bodies occupy space and how to frame a dialogue.*

How bodies occupy space tells the audience everything about the relationships between characters before a word is spoken. Who moves and who receives movement. Who is centred and who is at the edge of frame. Movement is power — the character who initiates movement controls the scene. Stillness is either strength or submission depending on context. Every blocking decision is a statement about the power relationship.

The single vs the two-shot is the most fundamental framing decision in a dialogue scene. A two-shot is democratic — it gives equal weight to both people and lets the audience choose where to look. A single is dictatorial — the director controls exactly whose interior state the audience is inside. Singles allow emotional asymmetry: hold person A for two seconds, person B for five — that difference in duration is itself an argument about whose inner experience matters more right now.

- **Two-shot** — These people share a world. Use when the physical relationship between two bodies is the scene's meaning, or when you want to give the audience a moment of relief and orientation.
- **Singles** — We are inside separate interior worlds. The natural grammar of counterpoint — isolates each layer of the performance. Faster and cheaper to shoot. Gives complete editorial control in post.

**Rule ▸** Default to singles when the scene is about what two people are thinking. Default to two-shots when the scene is about what two people share.

### 23 · The master shot — when to break it
*Category: Scene & Film Architecture · Use when: deciding the moment to cut from wide to close.*

The master shot establishes a scene in wide before fragmenting into close coverage. The moment you break from the master into close-up is the moment you tell the audience this specific beat matters above all others. Breaking too early removes that signal — if every moment is emphasised, no moment is emphasised. Breaking too late means the audience has been denied the emotional depth of close coverage at the moment they needed it. The discipline is to stay in the master until the scene's most important emotional beat arrives — then cut in.

**Rule ▸** Before shooting, identify the one moment in the scene that earns the break from master to close-up. Everything before it stays wide.

### 24 · Spatial geography — establish before you fragment
*Category: Scene & Film Architecture · Use when: before cutting into close detail in any space.*

Before cutting between details meaningfully, the audience needs to know where everything is in space. If they don't have the geography of a room, a cut to a close-up of a hand is disorienting rather than precise. Hitchcock almost always establishes spatial geography early — often with a wide shot that orients the audience — and then fragments into close detail once the map is in their heads. Violating spatial geography is one of the most common mistakes in short films made by directors who are in a hurry to get to the close-ups.

**Rule ▸** The audience needs the map before the detail. One wide establishing shot at the beginning of a scene earns you every close-up that follows.

### 25 · The establishing shot — and when to violate it
*Category: Scene & Film Architecture · Use when: a character's disorientation is the scene's subject.*

The establishing shot is the grammar. Knowing it well enough to withhold it deliberately is the advanced tool. When Hitchcock drops the audience into a space without orientation, the resulting disorientation is not a mistake — it is the scene's first emotional statement. A character who wakes up and does not know where they are is best served by a camera that also does not know where it is. Spatial disorientation externalises psychological disorientation. The rule must be understood before it can be broken with precision — otherwise violation is just confusion.

**Rule ▸** Only withhold the establishing shot when the character's disorientation is the scene's subject. Know exactly why you are breaking the rule.

### 26 · Scene entry and exit points
*Category: Scene & Film Architecture · Use when: deciding where a scene starts and stops.*

Most beginner directors enter a scene too early and leave too late. Entering too early means the audience watches setup and logistics before the scene begins. Leaving too late means the audience watches the scene resolve and tidy itself up before the cut. Hitchcock almost always enters at the last possible moment — the scene is already happening when we arrive — and cuts away before the resolution. The audience's imagination completes both ends. What is imagined is always more powerful than what is shown. The scene's entry and exit points are editing decisions with enormous impact on rhythm, pacing and the audience's sense of a living, continuing world.

**Rule ▸** Find the latest possible moment to enter a scene and the earliest possible moment to leave it. The audience will complete both ends themselves.

### 27 · Pacing and rhythm across the whole film
*Category: Scene & Film Architecture · Use when: sequencing scenes across the whole film.*

Individual scene tools are well covered above. But the rhythm of the whole film — how scenes of different emotional temperatures are sequenced, how the film breathes, how you control the audience's energy across 90 minutes or 15 minutes — operates at a macro level that individual scene decisions cannot address. A film of continuous intensity produces numbness. A film of continuous gentleness produces disengagement. The audience needs to be taken through a sequence of emotional temperatures — tension and release, intimacy and distance, speed and stillness — that feels like breathing. The director who controls this rhythm controls the audience's experience of the whole film, not just individual scenes.

For short films: a 12-minute film needs at least three distinct emotional temperatures. Map them before you shoot. Know where the film breathes and where it holds its breath.

**Rule ▸** Before shooting, map the emotional temperature of every scene in sequence. If three consecutive scenes have the same temperature, one of them needs to change.

## CATEGORY F — PRE-PRODUCTION THINKING

### 28 · The script-to-screen gap — finding a scene's visual DNA
*Category: Pre-production Thinking · Use when: first reading a scene, before any shot list.*

Everything in this document assumes you are on set making decisions. But the most important directing work happens before that — in how you read a script and translate it into visual decisions. A scene on the page is a description of events. A scene on screen is a visual experience. The gap between those two things is where directing lives. Reading a script as a director means asking not what happens but what does this scene want to be? What is its visual DNA? What category is it — dialogue with action, dialogue without action, action without dialogue? What is its job in the film's architecture — establishment, loading, suspense? What tool should be making its meaning? These questions are answered at a desk, not on set. A director who arrives on set without having answered them will default to coverage and lose authorship of the film.

**Rule ▸** For every scene, answer the four framework layers before you write a shot list. The shot list is the last step, not the first.

### 29 · Directing performance — the actor relationship
*Category: Pre-production Thinking · Use when: working with actors — staging is your first direction.*

All the visual grammar in this document cannot save a bad performance. For a beginner making short films — especially with non-professional or semi-professional actors — the actor relationship may be the most urgent practical challenge you face on set. Visual grammar and performance direction are not competing disciplines — they are two sides of the same coin. Hitchcock was famous for saying actors should be treated like cattle, but this was a public performance. In practice he cast with extraordinary precision and gave actors the exact emotional conditions they needed through staging and shot design rather than lengthy discussion. The camera position was his primary direction tool — an actor shot from below feels powerful without being told to act powerful.

This tool requires its own dedicated study. Key starting points: Judith Weston's Directing Actors, the work of Sanford Meisner as background, and watching interviews with PTA, Kore-eda and Cassavetes on how they work with performers.

**Rule ▸** Your staging and shot design are your first direction to the actor. Place them correctly in the space before you give a single verbal note.

**Practical addition — playable actions.** On set, give the actor a *verb to play*, not a feeling to feel: *to hide, to test, to seduce, to punish, to reassure, to escape.* A feeling ("be sad") is not playable; an action is. Casting is roughly 80% of performance — cast precisely and most of the work is done before a note is given.

## ADDITIONS — CRAFT FOUNDATIONS (30–34)

> These were missing from the original document and are added so the workflow stands on a complete base. They are written in the same spirit as the tools above.

### 30 · The dramatic spine (Layer 0)
*Category: Pre-production / Writing bridge · Use when: the very first question on any scene.*

Before scene type, before any visual choice, name the drama: **what does each character want, what stops them, and what changes?** Every tool in this document is a *means*; the spine is the *end they serve*. A beautifully composed scene with no want is dead. As a writer this is home turf — the discipline is to refuse to make a single visual decision until the spine is explicit. This is the bridge between the writing document and this one.

**Rule ▸** No camera decision until you can state, in one line, what the scene's protagonist wants and what stands in the way.

### 31 · Composition fundamentals
*Category: Composition · Use when: framing any shot, before reaching for the advanced tools.*

The advanced composition tools (frame-within-frame, deep space, height) sit on top of a floor most beginners skip:
- **Rule of thirds** — place subjects and horizons off-centre on the third-lines for natural balance; centre deliberately for confrontation, symmetry or unease.
- **Headroom** — space above the head. Too much diminishes; too little traps.
- **Lead room / nose room** — leave space in the direction a subject looks or moves. Removing it creates tension.
- **Balance and negative space** — empty space is not wasted; it isolates, and it can carry as much meaning as the subject.

**Rule ▸** Learn the floor so the advanced tools have something to stand on. Break these rules only on purpose.

### 32 · Camera movement as meaning
*Category: Composition / Cutting · Use when: deciding whether — and why — the camera should move.*

Movement is one of the largest expressive levers and the most easily wasted. Each move means something:
- **Locked-off (no movement)** — stability, observation, or entrapment (no rescue).
- **Slow push-in** — psychological intensification; we are moving closer to a realisation.
- **Pull-out** — abandonment, scale, isolation; the world grows, the character shrinks.
- **Handheld** — immediacy, instability, subjectivity, documentary truth.
- **The oner / moving master** — see Tool 10; continuous revelation through layered space.
- **Dolly zoom** — the ground shifts under a character's understanding (the "vertigo" effect).

Movement should be **motivated** — by a character's attention, a shift in power, a revelation — not decorative.

**Rule ▸** Before moving the camera, name what the movement *means*. If it means nothing, lock it off.

### 33 · Continuity grammar — the 180° line, eyelines, cutting on action
*Category: Scene & Film Architecture · Use when: shooting coverage you intend to actually edit.*

These are the invisible rules a beginner breaks by accident and only discovers in the edit, when the footage refuses to cut together:
- **The 180° line** — keep the camera on one side of an imaginary line between two subjects, so screen direction stays consistent and they keep "facing" each other across cuts.
- **The 30° rule** — when cutting between two shots of the same subject, change the angle by at least 30° or the cut looks like a jump.
- **Eyeline match** — where a character looks in shot A must match what we see in shot B, and at the right height.
- **Cutting on action** — cut in the middle of a movement; the motion carries the audience across the cut and hides it.
- **Coverage** — shoot enough angles (master, singles, inserts, reactions) that you can actually build the scene in the edit. Knowing *when to break the master* (Tool 23) is useless if you didn't shoot the pieces.

**Rule ▸** Plan the line and the coverage before you shoot. The edit is only as free as the footage allows.

### 34 · Sound fundamentals + the J-cut and L-cut
*Category: Cutting & Time / Meaning-making · Use when: every scene — sound is half the film.*

Counterpoint (Tool 08) is the *advanced* use of sound. The floor beneath it:
- **Dialogue intelligibility** — audiences forgive a flawed image but never bad audio. Protect production sound above almost everything.
- **Diegetic vs non-diegetic** — sound from inside the world (footsteps, a radio) vs. from outside it (score). Know which you're using and why.
- **Room tone & ambience** — the texture of a space; record it, and use it to make silence feel alive.
- **The J-cut** — the *sound* of the next scene/shot starts *before* we cut to its picture. Pulls us forward; the most-used editing tool in dialogue.
- **The L-cut** — the *picture* cuts but the *sound* of the previous shot continues. Lets us watch a listener while a speaker keeps talking.

**Rule ▸** Cut sound and picture separately. The moment you cut the image is rarely the moment you should cut the sound.

# PART 5 — REFERENCE

## DIRECTORS TO STUDY

### Global masters
- **Hitchcock** — The grammar of suspense, counterpoint, the three-way game and the Kuleshov cut used as a weapon. Start: Rear Window, Vertigo, Psycho, The Birds.
- **Kubrick** — Form as content — every frame is an argument. Geometry, one-point perspective, camera height and power. Start: 2001, The Shining, Barry Lyndon.
- **Tarkovsky** — Stillness as weight, time as material, the held shot as spiritual experience. Start: Stalker, Mirror, Andrei Rublev.
- **Welles** — Deep space composition, frame within a frame, power in a single image. Start: Citizen Kane, The Magnificent Ambersons, Lady from Shanghai.
- **Leone** — Time dilation, silence as pressure, the close-up as psychology. Start: Once Upon a Time in the West, The Good the Bad and the Ugly.
- **Eisenstein** — Montage theory — the cut as collision creating meaning. Start: Battleship Potemkin, October.
- **Bong Joon-ho** — Loading disguised as other genres, pacing and rhythm across whole films. Start: Parasite, Memories of Murder, Mother.
- **Wong Kar-wai** — Colour and light as emotion, subjective time, negative space. Start: In the Mood for Love, Chungking Express.
- **Spielberg** — Fast identification, clear stakes, the oner as simultaneous parallel action. Start: Jaws, Schindler's List, Munich.
- **Fincher** — Clinical camera as dread, withholding as uncertainty, spatial geography as control. Start: Zodiac, Seven, The Social Network.

### New wave and contemporary
- **Paul Thomas Anderson** — The most Hitchcockian working director. Every scene runs counterpoint. Holds shots longer than anyone today. Start: Phantom Thread, There Will Be Blood, The Master.
- **Céline Sciamma** — Restrained counterpoint, deep space, the minimum visible signal. Start: Portrait of a Lady on Fire, Girlhood.
- **Lynne Ramsay** — Pure cinema — removes exposition almost entirely, trusts image and sound completely. Start: We Need to Talk About Kevin, You Were Never Really Here, Ratcatcher.
- **Yorgos Lanthimos** — Extreme counterpoint — flat affect-less language against operatic action. The gap is the film's entire texture. Start: The Favourite, The Lobster.
- **Barry Jenkins** — Studies faces the way Hitchcock did — holds close-ups until you see the thought behind the expression. Start: Moonlight, If Beale Street Could Talk.
- **Hirokazu Kore-eda** — Nobody working today is better at the gap between what family members say and what they mean. Start: Shoplifters, Still Walking, After Life.
- **Robert Eggers** — Deep space composition and loading — entire first halves before anything overt. Start: The Witch, The Lighthouse, Nosferatu.
- **Payal Kapadia** — The most important recent Indian film for this work — all tools operating for a specifically Indian audience. Start: All We Imagine as Light (2024).

### Indian masters
- **Satyajit Ray** — The most complete Indian filmmaker. Silence, faces, domestic tension. Start: Charulata, Apur Sansar, Nayak, Pather Panchali.
- **Mani Ratnam** — Tamil emotional architecture — songs as loading sequences, romance and violence in the same visual language. Start: Nayakan, Roja, Kannathil Muthamittal.
- **Balu Mahendra** — Available light, the camera as witness, Tamil naturalism. Start: Moodu Pani, Veedu, Nool Veli.
- **K. Balachander** — Counterpoint in Tamil cinema — characters saying one thing, meaning another. Start: Aval Appadithan, Nool Veli, Ninaithale Inikkum.
- **Vetrimaaran** — Dread from procedural filmmaking — no music cues, no tricks, just staging and editing. Start: Visaranai, Vada Chennai, Aadukalam.
- **RGV — early work only** — Handheld camera, low angles, claustrophobia, sound as aggression. Post-2005 decline is equally instructive as a cautionary study. Start: Shiva (1990), Satya, Company.
- **Adoor Gopalakrishnan** — Space and architecture as psychology, social constraint as suspense. Start: Elippathayam, Swayamvaram.
- **Girish Kasaravalli** — Social constraint as suspense without genre mechanics. Start: Ghatashraddha, Tabarana Kathe.
- **Shankar** — Spectacle and mass audience manipulation — expectation, interval blocks, the reveal. Study separately from the art cinema tradition.

## GLOSSARY (plain language)

- **Beat** — the smallest unit of a scene; a single moment of change.
- **Blocking** — where actors stand and move in the space.
- **Counterpoint** — image and dialogue saying different things at once.
- **Coverage** — the set of angles shot for a scene so it can be edited.
- **Diegetic / non-diegetic sound** — sound from inside the story world / from outside it (e.g. score).
- **Eyeline** — the direction a character is looking.
- **J-cut / L-cut** — sound of the next shot starts early (J) / sound of the previous shot continues after the picture cuts (L).
- **Master shot** — a wide shot covering the whole scene, used as the base.
- **Oner** — a scene (or long stretch) shot in one continuous take.
- **Single / two-shot** — one person in frame / two people in frame.
- **180° line** — the imaginary line you keep the camera on one side of to preserve screen direction.
